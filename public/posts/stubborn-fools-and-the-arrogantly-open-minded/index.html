Stubbornness, Open-mindedness, and Intellectual Arrogance

Written on Oct 4, 2026

Bob is discussing politics with his family over dinner. He makes a claim about the cost of a border wall. His nieces and nephews question it and offer reasons for thinking he is mistaken, but he dismisses them without listening. He regards them as people who do not know what they are talking about.

This is the opening case in Laura Frances Callahan’s Stubborn Fools and the Arrogantly Open-minded, published in the Canadian Journal of Philosophy in 2025. Bob’s behaviour gives a familiar picture of intellectual arrogance: confidence in a belief, resistance to criticism, and contempt for those who disagree.

The paper asks whether confidence and resistance explain his arrogance. Someone might refuse to change a belief because they are confused, exhausted, or attached to a friend. Someone else might readily abandon a belief because the new position makes them feel intellectually superior. Callahan uses these possibilities to argue that holding firmly to a view and being intellectually arrogant are different characteristics.

Confidence and disagreement 

Much of the background to this argument comes from the philosophy of disagreement. Suppose two people have considered the same evidence and reach different conclusions. Each has reason to regard the other as equally competent and well informed. Such a person is called an epistemic peer: a peer with respect to knowledge and reasoning about the particular question.

One response is to become less confident. The other person’s disagreement may indicate that one has overlooked something or assessed the evidence poorly. Philosophers call approaches favouring this response conciliationist. A steadfast response retains the original belief or confidence despite the disagreement.

Callahan observes that discussions of this problem often associate conciliation with humility and steadfastness with arrogance. Some psychological measures of intellectual humility also emphasise willingness to reconsider opinions, accept possible error, and change a view when new information arrives (2025, pp. 2–4).

She calls the underlying picture Hold Fast: intellectual arrogance consists in a tendency to be excessively confident or excessively resistant to changing one’s beliefs. It is a reconstruction of an influential assumption across these discussions, rather than a theory that all the philosophers and psychologists she cites have explicitly defended in that form.

On this picture, refusing to revise a belief is evidence of arrogance because arrogance is understood in terms of excessive confidence or resistance. Callahan’s counterexamples challenge the proposed identification in both directions. Some stubborn believers need not be arrogant; some arrogant people display their arrogance while changing their minds.

The stubborn believer who is not arrogant 

The first set of cases concerns people Callahan calls “stubborn fools.” They may reason badly or retain beliefs they ought to reconsider. Her claim is that these failures need not arise from intellectual arrogance.

Jim, for example, believes that a roulette wheel must soon produce black because it has not done so for several spins. This is the gambler’s fallacy: treating a run of previous outcomes as making a particular result due on the next independent spin. Jim is too confident, and he may remain convinced even after someone explains the mistake.

There are several possible explanations for his persistence. He might fail to understand the explanation, lack the relevant concepts, or be too tired to follow the reasoning. He might be preoccupied with the prospect of winning. The case does not require him to think himself superior to the person correcting him. Callahan therefore treats his mistake as a possible instance of irrationality without arrogance (pp. 5–6).

A second case concerns someone who values acquiring true beliefs much more than avoiding false ones. Callahan imagines an extreme believer, Saf, who tries to believe almost everything. Saf would accumulate errors and show little concern about contrary evidence. That unusual attitude towards error would not, by itself, establish a concern with intellectual superiority.

Relationships provide another source of resistance. A person may be slow to accept evidence that a close friend has behaved dishonestly. The reluctance could arise from affection and loyalty. Callahan leaves open whether friendship justifies this partiality. Even if it is a failure to respond properly to evidence, it need not be a failure of arrogance (p. 6).

Finally, people sometimes retain beliefs because reopening a question would consume time and attention needed elsewhere. Stable beliefs help organise decisions and action. Faith commitments are one example discussed in the paper, but the point extends to ordinary beliefs used to navigate daily life. Callahan does not claim that every such refusal to reconsider is reasonable. She argues that practical reasons for keeping a question closed are possible without a desire to defend one’s intellectual importance (pp. 6–7).

These cases separate the assessment of a belief from the assessment of the person’s intellectual character. A belief may be poorly supported; the believer may be careless or confused. Calling the failure arrogant adds a further claim about its source.

Callahan considers the objection that refusing to engage already treats other people’s views as unimportant. Her reply distinguishes avoidance from a judgment of superiority. Someone overwhelmed by a disagreement may set it aside without concluding that the other participants are inferior thinkers. Even a mistaken judgment that another person lacks relevant competence can arise from misleading information rather than pride (pp. 7–9).

Arrogance in changing one’s mind 

The second group of cases concerns belief revision itself. Sam has accepted since childhood that fluoride benefits teeth. She follows online material claiming otherwise and quickly adopts a conspiracy explanation. In Callahan’s vignette, Sam is attracted to the thought that she has independently uncovered something most people have missed. The claims about fluoride belong to the fictional sources Sam accepts; the example concerns her reasons for accepting them.

Gio was raised in a nonreligious household. At university, he wants to distinguish himself from the secular crowd and becomes a Christian. He presents his conversion as having escaped the errors of his upbringing and regards himself as especially capable of answering opponents (pp. 9–10).

Both characters abandon earlier beliefs. Their willingness to revise nevertheless serves a desire to see themselves as unusually perceptive or independent. Callahan locates the arrogance in that process of revision, as well as in any resistance they later display towards criticism.

The comparison becomes clearer in the alternative versions she gives. Another Sam inherits a conspiracy belief in childhood and avoids examining it. Another Gio retains the Christianity in which he was raised because it structures his life and he cannot imagine giving it up. These characters may also resist evidence. But their descriptions do not contain the same attempt to gain intellectual distinction through rejecting a previous outlook.

The comparison is not intended to settle the truth of either religious or nonreligious belief. Nor does it establish that inherited beliefs are preferable to beliefs acquired through reflection. It concerns the difference between arriving at a position for reasons connected with status and retaining that position for other reasons.

Callahan also qualifies the expression “open-minded.” Here it refers to readiness to abandon an existing belief. She does not commit herself to the claim that Sam and Gio possess open-mindedness as a genuine intellectual virtue (p. 9, note 16). A willingness to change a view is the behaviour being examined; whether that change is well motivated remains a separate question.

Attention to the intellectual ego 

Callahan’s alternative draws on her earlier no-distraction account of intellectual humility. On that account, humility involves freedom from prideful distraction by one’s intellectual ego. The person’s attention is available for the argument, evidence, or question, without repeatedly being diverted towards their own importance (2025, pp. 11–12).

A person at a philosophy talk might spend so much effort devising an impressive question that they stop following the speaker. A researcher might be preoccupied with how intelligent their writing appears. A reader might assess an argument through its consequences for their reputation, giving less attention to the reasons it offers. These are examples of intellectual activity distorted by concern with the self.

The distraction need not be deliberate or fully conscious. A person can seek confirmation of their intellectual standing without noticing how that concern shapes what they attend to or whom they dismiss.

Within this account, intellectual arrogance is one form of distracting pride. Callahan associates it with an inflated assessment of one’s merits and a concern with intellectual status that obstructs worthwhile inquiry. She distinguishes it from other forms of pride, such as vanity. She also qualifies “inflated”: it means inflated relative to the evidence someone has about their merits. An innocent mistake caused by misleading information about one’s abilities is not automatically arrogant (p. 12, note 20).

This account returns to Bob’s refusal to listen. His behaviour is arrogant because his concern with his own standing interferes with a fair response to his relatives. Jim’s misunderstanding of probability need not involve that concern. Sam and Gio, by contrast, can pursue intellectual importance through the very act of replacing their beliefs.

Losing status and acquiring it 

The paper explains these different responses through the significance a belief has for its holder. Some beliefs carry considerable personal investment. A scientist’s view after years of research may be closely connected with how she understands her intellectual achievement. A casually acquired belief about an unrelated subject may carry much less of that investment.

Callahan calls attention to the sense of ownership people have over beliefs. Someone who regards a conclusion as the product of their own judgment may experience a challenge as a criticism of their competence. Retaining the conclusion can then become a way of protecting their standing (pp. 12–13).

An inherited belief may offer a different opportunity. Giving it up can be represented as an achievement: the person has examined what they once accepted and reached a conclusion of their own. In Sam’s and Gio’s cases, this makes revision attractive to the intellectual ego. They can attribute the original mistake to their upbringing or information environment while claiming credit for overcoming it.

Callahan notes that their new positions still depend on testimony from other people. They have not literally established everything alone. What matters to the explanation is the more active role they perceive themselves as taking in accepting the new sources (p. 14, note 23).

A contrarian position may also offer membership in a supposedly discerning minority. A story about escaping deception can make the change feel like a victory over those who remain mistaken. This helps explain why a concern with intellectual status sometimes encourages revision rather than resistance (pp. 13–15).

The paper discusses psychological research connecting conspiracy beliefs with traits such as a desire for uniqueness, narcissism, and entitlement. Callahan treats these findings as support for the plausibility of some of her cases, while cautioning against relying heavily on individual studies. Some findings concern associations; only some bear on possible causal relationships. Her argument remains a philosophical analysis of the examples, not a new experiment or a claim about the motives of every person who accepts a conspiracy explanation.

The scope of the argument 

Callahan’s conclusion is that confidence and belief revision do not, on their own, determine intellectual humility or arrogance. The examples are hypothetical cases constructed to make differences in motivation visible. Their role is to test an account of arrogance, rather than to provide a quick method for classifying people from their outward behaviour.

She retains the possibility that arrogant people are stubborn, as Bob is. She also retains the possibility that reconsidering a belief is intellectually admirable. The argument concerns the proposed link between these behaviours and arrogance: it is not strong enough to identify the character trait with either holding firm or changing one’s mind.

This leaves room for humility alongside firm commitments. In her conclusion, Callahan mentions the moral equality of persons as a belief one might have reason to retain resiliently. An account that equated humility with a general readiness to revise would have difficulty accommodating such commitments (p. 15).

Her proposed alternative examines how a person conducts inquiry, including their attention, treatment of others, and interest in the status a conclusion offers. On this account, reconsideration can be distorted by pride just as persistence can. The difference between Bob and the arrogantly “open-minded” lies in the route each takes to intellectual importance.
