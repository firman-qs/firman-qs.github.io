“God is One” is a familiar statement in Islamic theology. At first, the word *one* seems straightforward. We use it every day to count things: one cup, one book, one person. Yet theologians have often meant something quite different when speaking of divine Oneness.

The fourteenth-century scholar Saʿad al-Dīn al-Taftāzānī affirmed that God is One while denying that God is a thing numbered or counted. That can sound puzzling. How can something be one without being countable?

Abbas Ahsan takes up this question in [*Theological Incompleteness; Beyond the Contradictory One Taftāzānī’s Gödel Inspired Solution*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10516-026-09784-7). His discussion brings together Islamic theology, Gödel’s incompleteness theorems, and forms of logic that allow conclusions to be revised. The connection takes some explaining, especially because a contradiction and an incomplete account are two different things.

## The trouble with “one”

Taftāzānī worked in the tradition of *kalām*, the use of reasoned argument to discuss theological questions. One of those questions concerns how language drawn from ordinary experience can describe God.

A cup can be counted because it belongs to a collection of possible cups. Calling it “one cup” tells us how many cups are present. The cup also has parts, such as its handle and body, although counting it as one does not tell us anything about those parts.

In the theological account Ahsan discusses, divine Oneness concerns the absence of constituent parts and of any divine partner or rival. God is not understood as a unit within a collection. Taftāzānī’s denial that God is *maʿdūd*—numbered or counted—belongs to this account of unity (Ahsan, 2026, §2).

The distinction is between numerical oneness and metaphysical unity. Numerical oneness answers a counting question. Metaphysical unity describes a way of being: without composition or a counterpart. Once that distinction is made, affirming divine Oneness does not require assigning God a place in a numerical series.

Ahsan knows that this distinction is already available in the tradition. His paper asks what happens if, for the sake of examining the reasoning, we temporarily insist on a numerical reading instead.

## An imposed contradiction

Suppose “God is One” is read as saying that God has a numerical property. Suppose we also retain the claim that God cannot be numbered. With those meanings fixed, the two claims conflict.

In logical notation, a contradiction has the form \(P \land \neg P\): a statement and its negation. They must concern the same thing in the same respect. A cup that is hot outside and cool inside presents no contradiction. Saying that the same part of the cup is both hot and not hot, at the same time and in the same sense, would.

Ahsan describes his numerical reading as a methodological stress test. The contradiction arises because he has imposed an interpretation that classical *kalām* rejects. He does not present it as a contradiction the tradition itself endorses (2026, §3; §9.7).

Restoring the distinction between the two meanings would remove the difficulty. Ahsan wants to give an account of how that choice is made: what reasons favour the metaphysical interpretation, and what rules allow the numerical interpretation to be set aside. He calls separating meanings at the outset *parameterisation*. His objection is to making that separation before examining the tension, rather than to every distinction between meanings (§9.6).

This is the setting for the paper’s discussion of logic. The theological commitments are already there. The proposed logical framework is intended to explain how they govern interpretation.

## Taftāzānī, the Liar, and Gödel

Taftāzānī also discussed the *Liar paradox*. Its familiar version is the sentence “This sentence is false.” If the sentence is true, then what it says makes it false. If it is false, its claim to be false appears true. Trying to assign it a truth value brings us back to the difficulty.

According to Ahsan’s account, Taftāzānī compared the paradox to an “irrational root” and treated its resolution as beyond human knowledge. Ahsan reads this as a willingness to leave a problem unsettled when the available reasoning does not resolve it (2026, §4).

Gödel provides a modern connection with limits on proof. An *axiom* is a starting assumption; a *proof* follows permitted rules to reach a conclusion from those assumptions. A formal system specifies its axioms and rules so that a proof can be checked mechanically.

There are several properties such a system might have. It is **consistent** if it cannot prove both a statement and its negation. It is **complete**, in the sense relevant here, if it can prove or disprove every sentence in its language. It is **deductively closed** if it includes all the consequences of its assumptions. A theory can be deductively closed while still leaving some questions undecided.

Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem, in the modern form incorporating Rosser’s improvement, concerns consistent systems with enough elementary arithmetic whose axioms can be listed by an algorithm. Such a system contains sentences that it can neither prove nor disprove. The second theorem concerns the system’s own consistency: under the relevant conditions and standard formalisation, it cannot prove its own consistency if it is consistent. The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/goedel-incompleteness/) explains the precise conditions.

For an analogy, imagine a rulebook for proving facts about whole numbers. Even if its rules avoid contradiction, they may leave some arithmetical questions unsettled. Adding a suitable axiom can settle a particular question. But an effective extension that remains consistent and sufficiently strong will still be incomplete.

These theorems do not establish a theological claim. They also do not show that classical logic is inconsistent, or that theology must use another logic. Ahsan acknowledges those limits, particularly in his replies to objections (§9.3; §9.13).

His use of Gödel is an analogy about the limits of derivation. Taftāzānī’s treatment of paradox and Gödel’s results belong to very different contexts, but Ahsan connects them through the possibility of disciplined reasoning that leaves some matters unresolved. The particular rules he proposes for theology have a separate motivation: representing competing interpretations and allowing an inference to be withdrawn.

## When further information changes a conclusion

Suppose someone tells you there is a bird in the garden. You might expect it to fly. Then you learn that the bird is a penguin, and you give up that expectation. You still accept the original information that it is a bird.

An expectation of this kind is a *default*. It applies ordinarily, but further information can defeat it. Reasoning with defaults is therefore called *defeasible reasoning*.

Classical deductive consequence works differently. If a conclusion follows from a set of premises, adding more premises does not stop it from following. This property is called *monotonicity*.

If we had assumed that every bird flies, discovering a penguin would give us a reason to revise that assumption. The deduction from “every bird flies” and “this is a bird” would still be valid. A default, by contrast, allows the expectation to be withdrawn while retaining the earlier information.

Ahsan uses non-monotonic reasoning to represent this latter pattern. In the theological case, the competing inferences concern how to read the word “One.” Other commitments can favour one reading and defeat another (2026, §7).

The framework he uses is called *preferential semantics*. A model is a possible interpretation in which statements have truth values. Classical consequence asks whether a conclusion holds in every model satisfying the premises. Preferential consequence considers the models ranked as most preferred. The ranking must be specified; it cannot simply mean choosing whichever interpretation seems agreeable.

## The grounds for preferring an interpretation

Ahsan takes three commitments from Taftāzānī’s theology: **non-composition**, meaning that God has no constituent parts; **non-quantification**, meaning that God is not a quantity or thing numbered; and **uniqueness of deity**, meaning that God has no divine partner or rival.

These commitments supply the reasons for ranking interpretations. The metaphysical reading of “One” fits them. The numerical reading conflicts with non-quantification. In Ahsan’s ordering, uniqueness takes priority, followed by non-quantification and then non-composition (2026, §8).

The paper expresses the selection of preferred models with a function called \(S\). A simplified notation for its consequence rule is:

\[
\Gamma \mathrel{\mid\!\sim} \varphi
\quad\text{iff}\quad
S(\operatorname{Mod}(\Gamma)) \subseteq \operatorname{Mod}(\varphi).
\]

Here, \(\Gamma\) is the set of starting statements and \(\varphi\) is a proposed conclusion. \(\operatorname{Mod}(\Gamma)\) denotes the models satisfying the starting statements. The function \(S\) selects the preferred ones. The rule says that the conclusion follows preferentially when it holds in every selected model.

In the case of divine Oneness, selection favours the metaphysical reading. The numerical interpretation is defeated by the theological commitments used to rank the candidates. Ahsan connects this process with *tarjīḥ*, the weighing and preferring of reasons, and *tanzīh*, safeguarding divine transcendence.

The paper also considers objections to this account. One concerns the difference between a number and the object counted. Although \(1=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\), that identity does not show that everything counted as one has corresponding parts. An indivisible object could still be counted. Ahsan’s reply is that non-quantification does the decisive work: in his adopted theological account, numerical predication places God within a countable framework, even if it does not imply composition (§9.5).

Another objection concerns the ranking itself. If non-quantification is already a strict condition every admissible model must satisfy, why would a conflicting numerical interpretation remain available to be ranked? In §9.4, Ahsan distinguishes a broader space of interpretive candidates from the models selected through theological priorities. His reply treats numerical oneness as a candidate considered before selection, then rejected through the ordering. This distinction is needed to understand why he describes the process as preference rather than simple deductive exclusion.

Where equally preferred models disagree on another question, the framework can leave that question unsettled. This is part of what Ahsan means by theological incompleteness. He does not claim to have proved an incompleteness theorem for theology comparable to Gödel’s theorem for arithmetic.

## Reasoning in the presence of a contradiction

There is one further issue. Choosing a preferred interpretation does not by itself explain how to reason from statements that remain contradictory.

In classical logic, a contradiction entails any conclusion. This principle is called *explosion*. From \(P\), we can infer “\(P\) or \(Q\).” If \(\neg P\) is also available, that disjunction permits us to infer \(Q\), whatever \(Q\) happens to be.

A database might contain both “the laboratory is open” and “the laboratory is not open,” referring to the same time. We would usually investigate the conflicting entries while continuing to use the rest of the records. Treating the contradiction as a reason to infer any unrelated claim would make the database useless.

*Paraconsistent logic* permits reasoning with inconsistent premises without explosion. Using it does not require believing that contradictions are true. That further position is called *dialetheism*. The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s entry on paraconsistency](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/logic-paraconsistent/) discusses the difference.

Ahsan proposes an optional safeguard based on LP, the *Logic of Paradox*, and its variant LPm, which favours minimally inconsistent models and allows classical consequences to be recovered in consistent cases. If the imposed numerical reading leaves a live contradiction at some stage, this safeguard prevents it from licensing arbitrary conclusions (§8; §9.9). The preferred theological reading is intended to remain consistent.

Under the preferred interpretation, “God is One” expresses unity without parts or partners. The numerical reading has been considered and rejected on the grounds supplied by Taftāzānī’s theology. Other questions need not receive a definite answer if the preferred models leave them unsettled. This is the openness Ahsan seeks to represent with his framework, alongside the theological commitments it is designed to preserve.
